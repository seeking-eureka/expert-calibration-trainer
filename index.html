1. The Question This App Answers

Every risk quantification program runs on expert estimates: how often an event happens, how much it costs, how likely a control is to fail. If those experts are overconfident, every model built on their numbers is precise and wrong. Calibration is the habit of matching your stated confidence to how often you turn out to be right. It can be measured, and it improves with practice and feedback.

An expert is calibrated if, across everything they say they're 70% sure of, they're right about 70% of the time, and if their 90% ranges catch the true answer about 90% of the time.

2. How a Session Works

  • A session is several batches of questions, 4 batches of 10 by default. The app draws them at random from the bank, giving each batch the same mix of categories and difficulty. Two questions about the same event never appear in one session.
  • True/false questions ask you to pick True or False, then choose how sure you are: 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 90% or 99%. 50% means a pure guess.
  • Range questions ask for a low and high value that you're 90% sure contains the answer (or 80%, if you change the target). You can type shorthand: 4.4M, 1.2B, 38K, 9.1%, or -37.63.
  • Answers are revealed only at the end of each batch. Seeing them one at a time tends to cause short-term swings that don't last.
  • After each batch you get the answers, a one-line lesson and a source for each, and one coaching tip chosen from your results.

3. Turning Answers into Probabilities

Each true/false answer is a probability that the statement is true. If you pick True at 80%, you've said P(true) = 0.80. If you pick False at 80%, you've said P(true) = 0.20. Every score for true/false questions uses that number p and the outcome o (1 if the statement was true, 0 if false).

p = c if you answered True with confidence c p = 1 − c if you answered False with confidence c

The calibration curve uses a folded view of the same answers. It groups them by the confidence you chose and plots how often your chosen answer was right. A point below the diagonal means you were right less often than you claimed.

4. The Brier Score and What's Inside It

The Brier score is the average squared gap between what you said and what happened. Lower is better. A perfect, all-knowing forecaster scores 0. Answering 50% every time scores 0.25.

Brier = (1/N) Σ (pᵢ − oᵢ)²

Allan Murphy showed in 1973 that it splits exactly into three parts. Group the answers by the probability you gave, pk, and let ōk be how often the statement was true in group k, and ō the overall share of true statements:

Brier = Reliability − Resolution + Uncertainty Reliability = (1/N) Σₖ nₖ (pₖ − ōₖ)² how far your stated probabilities sit from reality (lower is better) Resolution = (1/N) Σₖ nₖ (ōₖ − ō)² how well your answers separate true from false (higher is better) Uncertainty = ō (1 − ō) how hard the questions were to begin with; you can't change it

Reliability is calibration. Resolution is knowledge: an expert who always says 50% is perfectly reliable but has zero resolution. Good experts are both reliable and discriminating.

With only 10 to 30 true/false answers, the reliability term is biased upward: even a perfectly calibrated expert will show some, simply from chance. Compare yourself with the well-calibrated simulated expert at the same session size.

5. The Log Score, and Why Confidence Stops at 99%

The log score charges you the negative log of the probability you gave to what actually happened. Lower is better. Answering 50% every time scores ln 2 ≈ 0.693.

Log score = (1/N) Σ −ln(probability you gave the actual outcome)

It punishes confident mistakes far more than the Brier score does. Being wrong at 90% costs 2.30. Being wrong at 99% costs 4.61. Being wrong at 100% would cost infinity, wiping out every other answer in the session. That's why the highest choice is 99%.

Real experts are almost never entitled to 100%. Even well-known facts get misremembered, and questions get misread. If you're tempted to say 100%, 99% says nearly the same thing while leaving room for the error you haven't spotted.

6. Range Hit Rate, and Why Small Samples Matter

The range hit rate is the share of your ranges that caught the true answer. For a 90% target, a calibrated expert lands near 90%. Untrained experts usually land well below that, because their ranges are too narrow.

With 24 ranges, the hit rate itself is uncertain. The app treats your true hit rate as unknown and updates a flat Beta(1, 1) prior with your hits h and misses m, the same Bayesian update used in the other Seeking Eureka apps:

True hit rate ~ Beta(1 + h, 1 + m) The card shows the middle 95% of that distribution.

If the whole 95% range sits below your target, you're very likely overconfident on ranges, not just unlucky.

7. The Winkler Score

Hit rate alone rewards silly ranges: "between 1 and a trillion" always catches the answer. The Winkler score (Winkler, 1972) charges you the width of every range, plus a penalty when you miss, scaled by how much you missed by. For a range [L, U] at target 1 − α and true answer y:

Winkler = (U − L) + (2/α)·(L − y) if y is below your range + (2/α)·(y − U) if y is above your range

At a 90% target, α = 0.10, so every unit you miss by costs 20 units of width. Lower is better. The best score comes from ranges as narrow as your knowledge allows, and no narrower.

Putting different units on one scale. A $5.5B question and a 9.1% question can't be averaged directly, so each range is scored on a common scale first:

  • Log-scale questions (money, counts) are scored in orders of magnitude, log₁₀. A range of $1M to $10M has width 1.
  • Year questions are scored in decades. A range of 1990 to 2000 has width 1.
  • Other questions are scored relative to the size of the true answer. A range of 6% to 12% around a true 9.1% has width 0.66.

The card shows the average score split into its width part and its miss-penalty part, so you can see which one is costing you.

8. The Cost of Overconfidence

This card turns your range hit rate into a loss-estimate error. Suppose you gave a 90% range of $1M to $10M for an annual cyber loss, and loss models fit a lognormal to it, as FAIR-style tools do. If your 90% ranges really catch the answer only 60% of the time, your true uncertainty is wider than you stated.

Stated: σ = ln(U/L) / (2·z₀.₉₅) z₀.₉₅ = 1.645 Actual: σ = ln(U/L) / (2·z(1+h)/2) h = your range hit rate 1-in-20-year loss = √(L·U) · exp(1.645·σ) 1-in-100-year loss = √(L·U) · exp(2.326·σ)

At a 60% hit rate, the 1-in-20-year loss is about $30M instead of $10M, three times higher, and the 1-in-100-year loss nearly five times higher. Hit rates below 20% are floored at 20%, so a handful of misses can't send the number to infinity. If your hit rate is at or above your target, the card says so, and it shows how much your ranges may overstate the tail.

This assumes your ranges are centered correctly and only too narrow. Real expert errors also include bias, which would make the picture worse, not better.

9. Simulated Experts

Simulated mode answers a full session as one of three personas, so you can explore the results without typing 40 answers:

  • Well-calibrated: confidence matches its actual chance of being right (86% on easy questions, 70% on medium, 56% on hard), and its ranges catch the answer at the target rate.
  • Overconfident: claims about 24 points more confidence than it has at first, and its 90% ranges catch only 42% of answers in batch 1. It improves with each batch, to show what training looks like.
  • Underconfident: claims 10 to 16 points too little and gives ranges wide enough to catch about 98.5% of answers.

Simulated answers follow the same rules as yours and go through the same scoring code. Each run draws new questions and new random errors, so results vary.

10. Things to Try

  • Run the Overconfident simulated expert, then the Well-calibrated one. Compare the calibration curves, the Winkler width and penalty, and the cost card.
  • Run the Underconfident expert and look at the Winkler score. It almost never misses, yet it can score worse than the calibrated expert because its ranges are so wide.
  • Take the test yourself with a 90% target. On your next session, switch to 80%. Many people find 80% ranges easier to set honestly.
  • Try the equivalent-bet test on a range question: would you rather win $1,000 if the answer falls in your range, or win $1,000 on a spin with a 90% chance of paying? If you'd pick the spin, your range is too narrow.
  • Load your own questions, such as your firm's past incidents, and run a session for your team.

Input rules

  • Batches run from 1 to 6, and questions per batch from 5 to 15. The true/false share runs from 0% to 100% in steps of 10%.
  • At least one category must be on, and the bank must hold enough questions of each type for the session. If it doesn't, the app tells you how many it has.
  • Range answers need both ends, with the low end below the high end. Money and count questions need values above 0.
  • Uploaded files need the columns id, type, question, answer. The type is binary or interval; binary answers are TRUE or FALSE. Optional columns: category, region, era, event_group, difficulty (easy, medium, hard), unit, scale (linear or log), explanation, source_name, source_url, source2_name, source2_url, verified_on, and score_scale (a custom divisor for the Winkler score). Rows with errors are skipped and listed.
